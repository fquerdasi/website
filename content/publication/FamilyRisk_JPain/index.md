abstract: "Children are at increased risk of developing chronic abdominal pain if a parent has an abdominal pain-related disorder of brain-gut interaction such as irritable bowel syndrome, suggesting family mechanisms of intergenerational pain transmission. However, patterns of family risk and resilience factors that predict children’s vulnerability for developing chronic abdominal pain remain unknown. In a sample of parents with an abdominal pain-related disorder of brain-gut interaction and their young child without chronic pain, this cross-sectional study identified subgroups representing different constellations of family risk and resilience factors. It also tested subgroup differences on two vulnerability markers for children’s future development of chronic abdominal pain: child emotional functioning and parental protective behavior. Baseline data from 212 parents and their 4–7-year-old child, participating in a randomized controlled trial testing a preventative intervention were included. Latent mixture modeling indicated a 3-group solution: (1) “High Socioeconomic Status, High Functioning” (55%) characterized by lower anxiety and depression and higher family resilience, (2) “Low Socioeconomic Status, High Pain Burden” (13%) characterized by higher pain catastrophizing and gastrointestinal symptoms, and (3) “High Socioeconomic Status, High Distress” (32%) characterized by higher parenting stress. Two of the three groups (“Low Socioeconomic Status, High Pain Burden” and “High Socioeconomic Status, High Distress” groups) showed lower child emotional functioning, and the “Low Socioeconomic Status, High Pain Burden” group also showed higher parental protective behavior, than the “High Socioeconomic Status, High Functioning” group. Results contribute to theoretical models of intergenerational pain transmission and help identify families who could benefit from preventative interventions."
